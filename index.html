The idea

Qi Baishi (齐白石, 1864–1957) — the village carpenter who became one of the most reproduced painters in modern China, and whose late work turned again and again to a handful of ink strokes for a shrimp — is credited with a line that has outlived most of his paintings:

太似则媚俗，不似则欺世，妙在似与不似之间。

Too much likeness panders to vulgar taste; too little deceives the world. The marvel lies between likeness and unlikeness.

It's usually read as advice about painting. It's really a claim about where recognition itself lives. Above, strokes one through three commit to nothing — a curved line, a point, a dot could be almost anything. Somewhere around stroke four or five, without any single stroke doing something different in kind, it becomes unmistakably a shrimp. Keep adding strokes past ten or so — outlining the silhouette, drawing every leg instead of implying a cluster of them, hatching in shading — and it doesn't get more like a shrimp. It gets stiffer. The gesture that made it alive gets buried under the detail that makes it correct.

Nobody told you which stroke count was which as you dragged. That's the point: the middle zone isn't a fact you can be handed, it's a judgement you make by looking, and it moves depending on what you're drawing.

Where else it shows up

The same curve applies wherever something stands in for something else: a caricature that's just a few exaggerated lines can be more recognisable than a careful likeness; an emoji face is three or four marks and unmistakably a face; a logo redrawn with every texture and gradient a real object has, usually reads as worse, not better, than a flat mark. Likeness and unlikeness aren't opposites with a correct amount of each — they trade off, and the interesting work is in the trade, not at either end.
